## 03 — MASTER STORY

### The dots are not the whales

A map covered in Orca observations looks instantly understandable.

It is not.

Each point begins with something much smaller: a report, a sample, a survey, a photograph, a research record. Different people, organisations and datasets observed Orcas at different times and under different conditions. When those records are brought together, they can reveal where people have documented Orcas — but they do not become a live track, a migration route or a population count simply because they appear on one map.

That distinction is the reason 4PLANET is building SPECIES and ATLAS together.

Start with Orca as a living species: Orcinus orca, the largest member of the dolphin family. Then open the source layer. GBIF provides a canonical taxonomic identity and biodiversity records. OBIS aggregates marine occurrence datasets. NOAA Fisheries provides bounded species and population-specific context.

The useful part is not the number of dots. It is being able to move from a species to a source record, from a record to a place, and from a place back to the limits of what the evidence actually establishes.

This is an early example of Living Planet Intelligence: life first, data second, sources always available.

Explore Orca in 4PLANET SPECIES. Then open the same living world in ATLAS.

## 09 — NEWSLETTER VERSION

Subject working line: The dots are not the whales

This week we are using Orca to demonstrate a principle behind 4PLANET: a map should never become more certain than its sources.

Public biodiversity systems contain thousands of Orca records across many datasets. Those records can be extraordinarily useful. They can also be misunderstood.

A reported occurrence is not a live animal position. A cluster of observations is not a population estimate. Separate observations do not automatically form a migration route.

4PLANET SPECIES gives the Orca a persistent identity. ATLAS connects that identity to geographic records and source context. The goal is simple: make planetary information easier to explore without stripping away the evidence boundaries that make it trustworthy.

## 10 — LINKEDIN-NATIVE VERSION

Most environmental data products have the same design problem:

The visual becomes more confident than the evidence.

Put enough Orca observations on a map and the human brain immediately starts seeing population, range, movement and “where they are”.

But an occurrence record may establish only this:
someone recorded Orcinus orca at a place, at a time, through a particular dataset.

That is already useful.

We do not need to inflate it.

We are using Orca as one of the first proofs for 4PLANET SPECIES + ATLAS:
life-first identity → source records → geographic context → visible limitations.

The product should help a non-specialist explore serious planetary data without turning uncertainty into decoration.
